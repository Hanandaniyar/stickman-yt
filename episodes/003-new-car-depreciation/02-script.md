## Narration

### Hook: the vanishing money

A brand-new car. Shiny paint. That new-car smell. The stickman signs the papers, grabs the keys, and rolls toward the gate.

Above the car floats a big price tag. The front wheels cross the gate. And the number on the tag drops. By thousands.

Same car. Same paint. Same smell. Nothing changed in the last ten seconds.

So where did the money go?

### The mystery

Let's treat this like a crime scene. Yellow tape. A magnifying glass. One very confused stickman.

Here's what we know. In October 2026, the average new vehicle in America sold for a record $51,109 [S1]. That's a lot of money to watch melt.

So who took it?

### Wrong guess #1: the dealer kept it

First suspect: the dealer. Maybe the money stayed behind at the gate.

Nope. The dealer got paid once. The sale is done. Nobody at the dealership is collecting a bonus every time your wheels turn.

Suspect cleared. He goes back to his coffee.

### Wrong guess #2: the car got worse

Second suspect: the car itself. Maybe ten seconds of driving wore something out.

Let's check. Tires? Fine. Engine? Fine. Cup holders? Still holding cups.

Physically, it's the same machine it was a minute ago. The car didn't change.

(beat) But something did.

### Clue #1: the biggest cost isn't gas

Let's dust for fingerprints. Every year, AAA adds up what it really costs to own and drive a new car.

In 2026, the average came to $12,863 a year [S2]. That's about $1,072 a month [S2].

Now picture that as a pie. Most people guess the biggest slice is gas. It isn't.

The biggest slice is depreciation. About $4,422 a year [S2]. That's more than a third of the whole pie [S2].

Insurance is a slice. About $2,098 [S2]. Loan interest is a slice. About $1,184 [S2]. But depreciation is the big one.

Depreciation is just a fancy word for "the value quietly leaving." No receipt. No bill in the mail. It's like a wallet with a hole in the bottom. About $368 drips out every month [S2], whether you drive to work or just stare at the car from the window.

So we know the money leaks. But we still don't know why it leaks so fast at the very start.

### Clue #2: what does the next buyer know?

Here's the key question. The moment you drive home, you're not a buyer anymore. You're a future seller.

And the next person to buy your car has a problem. They can't see inside it.

Was it babied? Was it rained on, slammed into curbs, driven like a rental? You know. They don't.

That gap, between what the seller knows and what the buyer knows, is our real suspect.

### The answer: the market for lemons

In 1970, an economist named George Akerlof wrote a paper called "The Market for Lemons" [S3].

In car talk, a lemon is a car with hidden problems. Looks great. Runs terrible. A sour surprise.

Akerlof's idea was simple. In the used-car market, sellers know more about quality than buyers do [S3].

Let's draw it. A parking lot full of used cars. Most are fine. A few are lemons. From the outside, they all look the same.

You're the buyer. You can't tell which is which. So what do you offer?

You offer a price that's somewhere in between. Not a "great car" price. Not a "lemon" price. A "maybe it's a lemon" price.

Now look at the owners of the great cars. They think, "My car is worth more than that." Some of them walk away. They keep their cars.

Who's left in the lot? More lemons. So buyers get even more careful. They offer even less. And more good cars leave [S3].

It's a slow slide. The bad cars push the price down for everyone [S3].

Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics for this kind of work on markets where one side knows more than the other [S3][S4].

Yes. A Nobel Prize. For lemons. (beat) Somewhere, a lemonade stand feels very validated.

### Back to the crime scene

So let's solve the case.

Inside the dealership, your car is "new." Everybody knows it has zero history. No secrets. No hidden dents. No mystery.

The second you drive it home, it becomes "used." Not because it's worse. Because now it has a history that only you know.

From that moment, every future buyer looks at it and thinks, "Hmm. Could be a lemon."

Your shiny car didn't turn into a lemon. It just joined the club of cars that might be.

That price drop at the gate? It's the price of missing information.

One honest footnote. Lemons aren't the whole story. Miles, wear, and newer models all pull values down too. And economists still test how big the lemon effect really is [S3]. But it explains the weird part: why value falls before the car has done anything at all.

### Bonus clue: the 84-month calendar

While we're at the crime scene, there's a second leak worth checking. The loan.

In the third quarter of 2026, the average new-car loan was a record $44,664 at about 7% interest [S5]. The average payment was $787 a month [S5].

And more than one in five new-car buyers signed up for $1,000 a month or more [S5].

To shrink that payment, a lot of people stretch the loan. A quarter of new-car loans now run 84 months or longer [S5]. That's seven years. Picture a wall calendar that just keeps unrolling down the hallway.

Here's the trick. Take that $44,664 at 7% [S5]. Over 60 months, the payment is about $884. Over 84 months, it drops to about $674 [D1].

Smaller payment. Feels like a win.

But total interest goes from about $8,400 to about $11,960 [D1]. The payment shrinks. The pile grows.

It's like ordering the "small" coffee that somehow costs more by the end of the week.

And there's a timing problem. Depreciation is fastest early. A long loan is paid down slowly early. So for a while, you can owe more than the car is worth. Two leaks. Same wallet.

### The daily view

Let's zoom all the way in. AAA's $12,863 a year works out to about $35.24 a day [S2].

Every day. Weekends too. Even on days the car never leaves the driveway.

Picture a $35 cup of coffee. Every morning. Whether you drink it or not.

(beat) That's not a car. That's a very expensive roommate who doesn't pay rent.

### What this means for you

So what can you do with this? Here are three ideas worth keeping. These are concepts, not personal financial advice. Your situation is your own.

One. Look at total cost, not the sticker or the monthly payment. A car's real price is the purchase plus depreciation, insurance, interest, gas, and repairs. The monthly payment is just the part that sends you a bill.

Two. Loan length has a price. A longer loan makes each month lighter and the whole trip heavier. Before you compare payments, compare total interest.

Three. Information has a price. The lemon discount exists because buyers can't see inside the car. Anything that lets them see more, like maintenance records or an independent inspection, can shrink that doubt. That works whether you're buying or selling.

### Wrap-up

So, case closed. The money didn't vanish at the gate. It was never really "in" the car. It was in the certainty that the car was new.

And certainty, it turns out, is one of the most expensive features on the lot.

Next time you see a shiny car roll out of a dealership, picture the little price tag above it. And maybe, just maybe, a lemon in the passenger seat.

## Fact notes (not narrated)
- [D1] Derived (our arithmetic, standard amortization): $44,664 at 7.0% APR. 60 months: $884.40/mo, total interest ≈ $8,400. 72 months: $761.48/mo, ≈ $10,162. 84 months: $674.10/mo, ≈ $11,960. Uses Edmunds' average amount financed and APR [S5]; it is an illustration, not a quote from Edmunds.
- $368/month = $4,422 / 12 = $368.50 (rounded down in narration). Depreciation share: $4,422 / $12,863 = 34.4% ("more than a third") [S2].
- $35.24/day is AAA's own daily figure as reported by CarPro ($12,863 / 365 = $35.24) [S2]. Monthly $1,071.92 ≈ $1,072 [S2].
- AAA assumptions: 5 years, 75,000 miles (15,000/year), gas $4.152/gal (12-month average through May 2026), sales-weighted average MSRP $39,376, 34 models. AAA changed methodology from 2025, so no year-over-year comparison is made in narration [S2].
- "More than one in five" = 21.2% with $1,000+/month payments. "A quarter" = 25.5% of new-car loans at 84 months or longer. Both Q3 2026, Edmunds via Click2Houston [S5].
- "Somewhere in between / maybe it's a lemon price" and "good sellers exit, leaving lemons": paraphrase of the Academy's summary of Akerlof's model (buyers pay at most the average valuation; high-quality sellers exit, leaving an adverse selection of low-quality goods) [S3].
- "Economists still test how big the lemon effect is": the Academy background lists empirical tests of the lemons model (e.g., Bond 1982, used pickup trucks) [S3]. No claim about the size of the effect is made.
- "The dealer got paid once" and "the car didn't physically change" are the script's logic for the hook scene, not statistics. "For a while you can owe more than the car is worth" is stated as a possibility (mechanism), not a statistic.
- Not used on purpose: any single "% lost when you drive off the lot" number (sources disagree), any coffee price, any brand, model, or dealer name, any "buy used instead of new" recommendation.
- The $35 coffee, the "small coffee" joke, the roommate line, and the lemonade-stand line are illustrative jokes, not factual claims.
