2. Perjalanan lewat 5 tempat: di dalam chip (chip hidup dari medan magnet terminal, membuat kode sekali pakai) → pusat data processor → "jalan tol" jaringan kartu (lelucon: biayanya memang bernama *interchange*) → aula bank penerbit (cek kartu, saldo/limit, penipuan) → kembali ke kafe: APPROVED.
3. Twist pertama: "approved" belum berarti uang pindah. Malamnya, saat kota tidur, terjadi *batch* dan *settlement*; uang baru tiba 1–3 hari kerja kemudian, dan potongan diambil sebelum masuk ke rekening kafe.
4. Kopi $5 dipotong-potong: rata-rata nasional 1,59% ≈ 8 sen (hitungan kami). Potongan terbesar ke bank penerbit (interchange), sedikit ke jaringan, sisanya ke processor. Totalnya $198,25 miliar setahun di AS (Nilson).
5. Twist kedua: toko tidak memasang harga berbeda, jadi biaya itu masuk ke harga semua orang, termasuk pembayar tunai. Studi Boston Fed (2010, ditandai sebagai studi lama): rumah tangga tunai rata-rata "membayar" $149/tahun ke pengguna kartu.
6. Penutup: 3 konsep (bukan saran personal): harga sudah termasuk biaya pembayaran; reward bukan uang gratis; "approved" itu janji, bukan pembayaran (hold mengurangi limit). Tanpa merek kartu/bank.

## Narration

### Hook: at the café counter (store-checkout)

A cup of coffee. Five dollars. You tap your card on the little screen. Beep. Approved.

That took about two seconds. In those two seconds, your coffee money took a trip across the country and back. And somebody took a cut along the way.

Let's follow it.

### Time freezes (store-checkout)

Snap. We freeze time.

The barista is frozen mid-yawn. The milk foam is frozen mid-air. A guy in line is frozen mid-sigh.

But one thing is still moving. A tiny glowing message, leaving your card. It says: "Somebody wants a coffee. Can they pay?"

That question gets answered in just a few seconds [S1]. It just has to visit a lot of places first.

### Stop 1: inside the chip (studio)

Let's shrink down and climb inside your card.

Here's the weird part. Your card has no plug. When you hold it near the reader, the reader makes a magnetic field. That field powers the chip and wakes it up [S3]. It only works up close, within about 4 inches [S3].

So your card is basically a tiny introvert. It sleeps all day, and only talks when someone gets really close.

Once awake, the chip does something clever. It doesn't just shout your card number. It makes a brand-new code, just for this one purchase [S2]. Your real card number never leaves the card [S2].

If a thief copies that code, it's useless. It's like a movie ticket that's already been torn [S2].

### Stop 2: the processor (data-center)

The message zips out of the terminal and lands in a giant data center. Rows of humming servers. Blinking lights. Very cold air.

This is the payment processor. Think of it as the café's mail room. The café doesn't know every bank in America. So it hands the message to the processor, which works with the café's own bank, called the acquirer [S1][S4].

The processor stamps it, checks the address, and sends it on to the card network [S1][S4].

No shoes off. No tiny shampoo bottles. Fastest security line in history.

### Stop 3: the card network (highway)

Now our message hits the highway.

The card network is a giant road system. Its job is to connect the merchant's bank to your bank [S4]. Millions of messages race down the lanes at once. One card network alone reported about 901 million transactions a day [S7].

The network looks at the card number and points the message to the right exit. Your bank.

Fun fact. Highways have interchanges. And the biggest fee in this whole story is literally called interchange [S4]. (beat) Keep that word in your pocket. We'll need it later.

### Stop 4: the issuing bank (bank-hall)

The message arrives at a grand bank hall. Marble floors. Tall columns. This is your bank, the one that gave you the card. It's called the issuer [S1][S4].

A very serious clerk opens the message and asks three questions [S1].

Is this card real, and still active? Is there enough money, or enough credit, for five dollars? And does anything here look like fraud [S1]?

Hmm. Coffee at 8 a.m. near your home. Seems normal. If you'd bought a jet ski in another country three minutes ago, the clerk might raise an eyebrow.

Today, everything checks out. The clerk stamps it: APPROVED [S1].

Then the bank sets five dollars aside, so you can't spend it twice. This is called a hold [S1].

### Back at the counter: approved (store-checkout)

The answer races back. Bank hall. Highway. Data center. Terminal [S1].

Beep. Time unfreezes. The foam lands in the cup. The barista finishes the yawn. The guy in line finishes his sigh.

You grab your coffee and leave. The whole trip took less time than it takes to say, "Can I get oat milk?"

But here's the twist. (beat) The café still doesn't have your money.

### That night: settlement (city-skyline)

"Approved" is a promise. It's not a payment. A transaction can be approved and still never settle [S1].

So let's skip ahead. The café closes. The city goes dark.

At the end of the day, the café sends all of its approved sales in one big bundle. That's called a batch [S6]. Every coffee. Every muffin. Every sad little banana.

Then the money actually starts moving, from your bank to the café's bank. That's settlement. It usually takes one to three business days [S6].

And when the money finally lands, it's not the full amount. The fees come off first [S6].

### Slicing the five-dollar coffee (paper-grid)

So how much comes off? Let's put the coffee on the cutting board.

In 2025, American merchants paid $198.25 billion in card processing fees [S5]. That's up from $187.20 billion the year before [S5].

On average, that works out to 1.59% of every purchase [S5]. On a five-dollar coffee, that's about 8 cents [D1].

Eight cents. Doesn't sound like much. But it happens on almost every sale, all day, every day.

Now, who gets the 8 cents? Three slices [S4].

The biggest slice goes to your bank. That's the interchange fee. Told you we'd need that word [S4].

A thin slice goes to the card network. Network fees are usually around 0.1% to 0.2% [S4]. On our coffee, that's a penny or less [D2].

And the rest goes to the processor and the café's bank, for running the machines and the mail room [S4].

Real fees vary a lot by card, by shop, and by size of sale. But that's the basic recipe.

### Who really pays: the price tag (supermarket)

Okay. So the café pays the fee. Problem solved, right?

Not quite. Walk into any store. Look at the price tags. There's one price. Not a card price and a cash price. Just one.

Stores can't make the fee disappear. So it can end up baked into the price, for everyone [S4].

Rewards come from this too. Cash back, points, miles. They're generally paid for with interchange money [S4]. Which comes from merchants. Which comes from prices.

### Who really pays: the cash customer (street)

Meet the cash guy. He pays with paper. He's getting rarer. Cash fell from 33% of payments in 2015 to just 14% in 2025 [S8]. Credit cards went from 18% to 34% [S8].

Since most stores charge everyone the same price, cash users help cover card fees and card rewards [S9].

A Boston Fed study back in 2010 tried to measure this. It found that, on average, each cash-using household paid about $149 a year to card-using households [S9].

(beat) So the cash guy is helping pay for someone else's airline miles. And what does he get? A receipt.

It's an older study, and the exact numbers have surely changed. But the idea still holds. When the price is the same for everyone, the cost is shared by everyone [S9].

### What this means for you (kitchen)

So what can you take home from this trip? Three ideas. These are concepts, not personal financial advice.

One. Every price already includes the cost of getting paid. Your coffee, your groceries, your phone bill. Somewhere in there is a small slice for the payment system. You pay it whether you notice it or not.

Two. Rewards aren't free money. They're funded by fees, and fees are built into prices [S4][S9]. And they only come out ahead if the card doesn't cost you more than it gives back, like through interest or annual fees.

Three. "Approved" is a promise, not a payment. A hold can tie up part of your balance or credit for a few days, and sometimes up to 31 days [S1]. That's why a hotel or gas pump hold can make your account look smaller than you expect.

### Wrap-up: back at the café (store-checkout)

So that's the trip. A chip that wakes up from a magnetic field. A data center. A highway. A bank hall. And back, in about two seconds.

Then a quiet night of paperwork, and a few cents shaved off along the way.

Next time you hear that little beep, picture the tiny glowing message racing home. And remember: someone in line just helped pay for the trip. (beat) Maybe even the guy with cash.

## Fact notes (not narrated)
- [D1] Derived (our arithmetic): $5.00 × 1.59% = 7.95¢ ≈ 8¢. Cross-check: $198.25B ÷ $12.498T purchase volume ≈ 1.586% [S5]. This is a national average across all cards and sales; real fees on a single small purchase can be higher because many pricing plans include a fixed per-transaction charge (CRS lists examples like "+$0.10") [S4]. Narration says "on average".
- [D2] Derived: 0.1%–0.2% of $5 = 0.5¢–1¢, so the narration says "a penny or less" (Handur fix).
- "A few seconds": Stripe says authorization "usually lasts just a few seconds" [S1]. "About two seconds" in the hook and wrap-up follows the owner-approved title; it is presented as a typical feel, not a measured statistic.
- "Within about 4 inches": ISO/IEC 14443 contactless cards work at less than 10 cm (4 inches) per the Secure Technology Alliance FAQ [S3]. "The field powers the chip" is from the same source. We do not state "no battery" as a fact; "no plug" is the visual line.
- "Your real card number never leaves the card" and "unique code for every purchase": Stripe EMV explainer [S2]. Tap and insert both use the chip.
- The route terminal → processor/acquirer → network → issuer → back, and the three issuer checks (valid card, funds/credit, fraud flags), are from Stripe [S1] and CRS [S4]. The jet-ski example is an illustrative joke, not a fraud rule.
- "One card network alone reported about 901 million transactions a day": Visa FY2025 annual report, shareholder letter (329 billion Visa-branded transactions, ~901 million per day, worldwide, processed by Visa or other networks) [S7]. Brand not named in narration.
- "The biggest slice goes to your bank (interchange)", "network fees usually 0.1%–0.2%", "rest to processor", "rewards generally paid for with interchange revenue", "merchant may pass the cost to consumers through higher prices": CRS IF11893 [S4]. CRS says pass-through "depends on market characteristics", so the narration says "can end up baked into the price".
- Batch at end of day, settlement 1–3 business days, net deposit after fees: Stripe settlement explainer [S6]. Holds "often clear within a few days but can last up to 31 days": Stripe [S1].
- Cash/credit shares (2015 vs 2025): Atlanta Fed Survey and Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, via Pew, Sep 30, 2026 [S8].
- $149 per cash-using household per year: Boston Fed Public Policy Discussion Paper 10-3, revised August 2010 (the original version said $151; we use the revised figure) [S9]. Narration flags it as an older study.
- "Interest or annual fees" in takeaway two is a general concept, no figures used.
- Not used on purpose: Regulation II and the debit fee cap (legal status unsettled), swipe-fee legislation, any card brand or bank name, any specific interchange rate table, any "pay with X card" recommendation.
