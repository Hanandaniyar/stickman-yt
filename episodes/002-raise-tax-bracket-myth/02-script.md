## Narration

### Hook

You just got a raise. Then a coworker leans over and says, "Careful. That raise could bump you into a higher bracket, and you'll take home less."

It sounds scary. It's also mostly wrong. Let's see why.

### The myth

Here's the fear in one picture. A stickman climbs one step up a staircase. Suddenly, every dollar he has gets taxed at the higher rate.

If that were true, a $1 raise could cost you thousands. Nobody would ever want a promotion.

But that's not how federal income tax brackets work. Not even close.

### First, the free bucket

Before any bracket kicks in, there's something called the standard deduction. Think of it as a tax-free bucket that fills up first.

For 2027, it's $16,600 if you're single. [S1] It's $33,200 for married couples filing jointly. [S1]

So if you earn $68,100 in salary as a single filer, the first $16,600 isn't taxed by the federal income tax at all. [S1] What's left, $51,500, is your taxable income. That's the number the brackets care about.

Quick note. This is the simple version. Real returns can have other deductions and credits. We're keeping it clean on purpose.

### How brackets actually work: the buckets

Now picture a row of buckets on a shelf. Each bucket has a tax rate painted on the side.

Your taxable income is water. You pour it in from the left. The first bucket fills up. Only then does water spill into the next one.

For a single filer in 2027, here's the row. [S1]

The first bucket is 10%. It holds the first $12,800. [S1]

The second bucket is 12%. It holds everything from $12,800 up to $52,025. [S1]

The third bucket is 22%. It starts above $52,025, and runs up to $109,125. [S1]

After that come buckets for 24%, 32%, 35%, and finally 37% above $661,375. [S1]

Here's the key. Each bucket only taxes the water inside it. The water in the 10% bucket stays at 10%. Forever. Even if the 22% bucket is overflowing.

Pouring more water into the next bucket doesn't change the rate on the earlier ones. Gravity doesn't work backwards. Neither does the tax code.

### The worked example

Let's run the numbers. Our single stickman has $51,500 of taxable income. [S1]

All of it fits in the first two buckets. His top bucket is 12%. His federal income tax is about $5,924. [S1]

Now he gets a $1,000 raise. His taxable income becomes $52,500.

Uh oh. The 22% line is at $52,025. [S1] He crossed it. Cue the dramatic music.

So what happens? The first $525 of the raise still lands in the 12% bucket. That's $63 in tax.

The other $475 spills into the 22% bucket. That's about $104.50 in tax.

Add them up. The raise costs about $167.50 in federal income tax. [S1]

So he keeps about $832 of his $1,000 raise, before payroll and state taxes. Not zero. Not negative. Just a bit less than the full thousand.

His old income? Taxed exactly the same as before. Nothing got re-taxed.

### Marginal vs. effective rate

This is where two terms help a lot.

Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar. It's the bucket the water is currently pouring into. For our stickman, that's now 22%. [S1]

Your effective rate is your total tax divided by your income. It's the average across all the buckets.

His total federal income tax on $52,500 of taxable income is about $6,092. [S1] Divide that by $52,500, and you get about 11.6%. [S1]

So he's "in the 22% bracket." But he's paying about 11.6% on his taxable income. Measured against his full $69,100 salary, it's even lower, about 8.8%.

Think of it like a restaurant bill. The last dish you ordered was the priciest. That doesn't make every dish on the table cost that much.

### Why the buckets keep getting bigger

Here's another quiet detail. The buckets aren't fixed. They grow a little each year.

The IRS adjusts the brackets for inflation every year. [S1][S2] In 2026, the 22% bracket for single filers started above $50,400. [S2] For 2027, it starts above $52,025. [S1] That's $1,625 higher.

The standard deduction grew too. From $16,100 in 2026 to $16,600 in 2027 for single filers. [S1][S2]

Why bother? Because without these adjustments, a raise that only kept up with prices could slowly push people into higher buckets. They'd pay a bigger share without actually being richer.

Indexing makes the buckets grow along with prices. So keeping pace with inflation doesn't automatically mean a bigger tax share.

### The honest exceptions

Now, to be fair, there are real cases where a raise can sting more than the brackets suggest.

Some benefits shrink or disappear as income rises. Things like help with food, housing, or health coverage. [S3]

Researchers call it a benefits cliff. In some cases, a small raise can cost a family more in lost benefits than the raise itself. [S3] That's a real issue. But it's about benefit rules, not tax brackets.

### What it means for you

So what does this mean for you?

First, crossing a bracket line only affects the dollars above that line. The money below it is taxed the same as before.

Second, when someone says, "I'm in the 22% bracket," that's their marginal rate. Their effective rate is usually much lower.

Third, a paycheck also has other deductions. Social Security, Medicare, and maybe state taxes. So your take-home change will be smaller than the raise. But the federal brackets alone won't flip a raise into a loss.

This isn't tax advice. Everyone's situation is different, and real returns have more moving parts.

### Conclusion

So, will a raise push you into a higher tax bracket? Maybe. And that's fine.

Only the new water goes into the new bucket. Everything you poured in before stays right where it was.

Next time your coworker panics about brackets, just picture the buckets. Then go enjoy your raise.

- 2027 single brackets (S1, re-verified 2026-11-12 against IR-2026-133): 10% to $12,800; 12% over $12,800; 22% over $52,025; 24% over $109,125; 32% over $208,325; 35% over $264,550; 37% over $661,375. MFJ standard deduction $33,200; single $16,600. All match `01-topics.md`.
- Salary example: $68,100 − $16,600 = $51,500 taxable; after a $1,000 raise, $69,100 − $16,600 = $52,500 taxable. The salaries are hypothetical illustrations (standard deduction only, no other deductions or credits).
- Tax on $51,500: $12,800 × 10% = $1,280; ($51,500 − $12,800) × 12% = $38,700 × 12% = $4,644; total = $5,924.
- Tax on $52,500: $1,280 + ($52,025 − $12,800) × 12% = $1,280 + $4,707 = $5,987; + ($52,500 − $52,025) × 22% = $475 × 22% = $104.50; total = $6,091.50 ≈ $6,092.
- Raise cost: $525 × 12% = $63.00; $475 × 22% = $104.50; total $167.50 (= $6,091.50 − $5,924). Kept ≈ $1,000 − $167.50 = $832.50 ≈ $832 (before payroll and state tax).
- Effective rate: $6,091.50 / $52,500 ≈ 11.60% of taxable income; $6,091.50 / $69,100 ≈ 8.82% of gross salary.
- Indexing: $52,025 − $50,400 = $1,625 (S1, S2). Standard deduction $16,100 → $16,600 (+$500, single) (S1, S2).
