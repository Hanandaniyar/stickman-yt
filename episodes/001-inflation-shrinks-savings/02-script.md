## Narration

### Hook

Your savings balance hasn't dropped a single cent. So why does it buy less every year?

Picture a stickman standing perfectly still on an escalator that's going down. He isn't moving. And yet, he's going down.

That's your money. Today, let's figure out how fast that escalator is moving, and what it means for you.

### The problem

Here's a simple test. Take $100 from September 2020. Now walk it into a store in September 2026.

Prices rose about 29.5% over those six years. [S4] So that same $100 now buys roughly what $77 bought back then. [S4]

The bill didn't change. It still says 100. But it quietly lost about $23 of shopping power. Nobody sent you a notice.

Go back 10 years, and it gets worse. $100 from September 2016 buys about $72 worth of stuff today. [S4]

### Why it happens

This slow leak has a name. Inflation. It just means the general level of prices is rising.

The government tracks it with the Consumer Price Index, or CPI. Think of it as a giant shopping cart. It holds the things households typically buy. Food, rent, gas, doctor visits, haircuts.

Every month, the Bureau of Labor Statistics checks what that cart costs. [S1] If the cart gets pricier, that's inflation.

So how fast is the cart getting pricier? As of September 2026, prices were up 3.7% over the past 12 months. [S1]

Some items jumped way more than others. Gasoline was up 34.2% in a year. [S1] Energy overall rose 20.4%. [S1] Food was up a calmer 2.5%. [S1]

Strip out food and energy, and you get "core" inflation. That was 2.6%. [S1] Economists watch it because gas prices swing around like a toddler on a sugar high.

Quick side note. The October report had no 12-month number, because October 2025 data wasn't collected during the government shutdown. [S2] So September is the latest full-year reading.

### How it works: relative motion

Okay, here's where a little physics helps.

In physics, motion is relative. A passenger on a train is sitting still compared to their seat. Compared to the ground, they're doing 80 miles an hour.

Your bank balance works the same way. Compared to your bank statement, it's standing still. Compared to prices, it's drifting backward.

Now, your savings account does pay some interest. So our stickman isn't just standing there. He's walking up the escalator.

The question is, how fast?

The national average savings rate is 0.38% a year, as of October 2026. [S3] That's our stickman taking tiny, polite steps.

Meanwhile, the escalator is heading down at 3.7% a year. [S1]

Here's the key idea. Economists call it the real interest rate. Roughly, it's the interest you earn, minus inflation.

The rate the bank advertises is the nominal rate. That's the number on the sign. The real rate is what actually happens to your buying power.

So, 0.38% minus 3.7%. That's about minus 3.2% a year. [S1][S3]

Let's make that concrete. Put $10,000 in an account paying 0.38%. After a year, you have $10,038. [S3] Nice. Your balance went up.

But to buy the same cart of stuff, you'd now need about $10,374. [S1]

So your $10,038 buys what about $9,676 used to buy. That's roughly $324 of purchasing power gone. [S1][S3] And not one dollar ever left the account.

### The faster walker

Now picture a second stickman. This one walks faster.

In early November 2026, some high-yield savings accounts advertised around 4% to 4.35% APY. [S5] APY means annual percentage yield. It's the yearly return, including compounding. Some of those top rates require a minimum balance. [S5]

That's roughly 10 times the national average. [S3][S5]

But at 4.25% against 3.7% inflation, the real return is only about plus 0.5%. [S1][S5]

So stickman number two is moving up. Barely. He's jogging hard just to stay in place, with a tiny bit of progress.

That's the surprising part. Even a much better rate mostly just keeps pace. It doesn't make anyone rich. It mostly stops the slide.

### Who controls the escalator?

So who sets the escalator's speed? Partly, the Federal Reserve.

The Fed aims for inflation of 2% over the long run. [S6] Right now, we're well above that. [S1]

One of its main tools is interest rates. In September 2026, the Fed raised its key rate. [S7] In October, it held that rate at 3.75% to 4%. [S7]

Higher rates make borrowing more expensive. That tends to cool spending, and over time, cool prices.

Higher rates can also nudge savings yields up. But here's the catch. The national average savings rate is still just 0.38%. [S3] That's far below the Fed's range. [S7]

### What it means for you

So what does this mean for you? A few ideas. No sales pitch.

First, a raise isn't always a raise. If your pay goes up 3% while prices go up 3.7%, your paycheck buys a little less than before. [S1] Your boss calls it a raise. Your shopping cart calls it a pay cut.

Second, when you see a rate, ask the real question. Not "what does it pay?" But "what does it pay compared to inflation?"

Third, cash still has a job. An emergency fund needs to be safe and easy to reach. It's not supposed to be a rocket. Just remember the escalator is running.

This isn't personal advice. Everyone's situation is different. The goal is simply to see the escalator clearly.

### Rule of 72

Let's finish with a neat little trick. It's called the Rule of 72.

Divide 72 by a yearly growth rate. You get roughly how many years it takes for something to double.

At 3.7% inflation, 72 divided by 3.7 is about 19.5. [S1] So if prices kept rising at September 2026's pace, they'd double in about 19 and a half years.

Imagine a $5 sandwich turning into a $10 sandwich. Same sandwich. Same pickles.

At the Fed's 2% target, doubling takes about 36 years. [S6] It still happens. Just slower.

### Conclusion

So here's the whole idea in one picture. Your balance isn't the full story. The escalator is always moving.

The number in your account tells you how many dollars you have. The real rate tells you whether those dollars are winning or losing.

Stand still, and you drift down. Walk, and you might hold your ground. Either way, now you know which way the stairs are moving.
